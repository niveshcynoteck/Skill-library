# The Colonial Era (1498–1947)

## Arrival of European Trading Powers

Portuguese explorer **Vasco da Gama** reached Calicut on India's Malabar Coast in **1498**, opening the sea route from Europe and establishing bases including Goa. The **Dutch** (first factory at Masulipatnam, 1605), the **British East India Company** (chartered 1600, bases at Surat, Madras, Bombay, Calcutta), and the **French** (Pondicherry) followed, competing for coastal trade through the 17th–18th centuries.

## Rise of the British East India Company (1757–1765)

- **Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757):** British forces under **Robert Clive** (~3,000) defeated Bengal nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah's ~50,000-strong army, aided by the defection of general Mir Jafar — widely regarded as the start of British territorial rule in India.
- **Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764):** Company forces defeated a tripartite alliance of Mir Qasim, Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, and the Nawab of Awadh.
- **Treaty of Allahabad (1765):** Granted the Company **Diwani** (revenue-collection) rights over Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, converting it into a territorial power.

## Transition to British Crown Rule (1857–1858)

The **Indian Rebellion of 1857** (Enfield rifle cartridge controversy, annexation of Oudh, broader discontent) prompted Parliament to pass the **Government of India Act 1858**, abolishing East India Company rule and transferring authority directly to the **British Crown**. The Governor-General was retitled **Viceroy of India** — the formal start of the **British Raj**.

## Key Policies of the British Raj

- **Land revenue systems:** Permanent Settlement (1793, Bengal/Bihar/Orissa, under Lord Cornwallis — fixed revenue, zamindars as landowners); Ryotwari (from c. 1820, Madras/Bombay — direct assessment on cultivators); Mahalwari (from 1819, Ganga valley/Punjab — village-level assessment).
- **Railways:** First railway opened 16 April 1853 (Bombay–Thane, 34 km); network expanded from 838 miles (1860) to 15,842 miles (1880), built mainly for troop movement and raw-material export.
- **Macaulay's education policy:** The "Minute on Indian Education" (2 February 1835) redirected funding toward English-language, Western-style instruction.
- **Deindustrialization:** British tariff policy plus machine competition devastated Indian textiles/handicrafts — India's share of world textile production reportedly fell from ~25% (17th century) to ~2% by the end of colonial rule.

## Socio-Economic Impacts

- **Famines:** The **Great Bengal Famine of 1770** killed an estimated 7–10 million people; the **Great Famine of 1876–1878** caused an estimated 5.6–9.6 million deaths, occurring even as colonial grain exports continued. Further million-plus-death famines occurred in 1896–97 and 1899–1900.
- **Drain of wealth theory:** Proposed by **Dadabhai Naoroji** (from 1867) and **R.C. Dutt** — Britain extracted wealth from India without adequate return (Naoroji estimated £30–40 million annually). **This is a contested interpretation**: contemporaries argued the payments compensated for genuine administrative/defense/infrastructure services; modern historians largely uphold the core insight while debating exact magnitudes.
- **Social reform movements:** **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** founded the **Brahmo Samaj** (1828) and campaigned against *sati*, leading to the Bengal Sati Regulation Act of 1829. The **Arya Samaj** (founded 1875 by Swami Dayanand Saraswati) promoted Vedic revivalism, widow remarriage, and opposed child marriage.

## Facts vs. Contested Interpretations

- **Well-established:** Dates of Plassey, Buxar, the 1858 Act, the 1835 education Minute, the 1853 railway opening, and reform-movement founding dates.
- **Contested:** Exact famine death tolls vary by source; the scale and net effect of the "drain of wealth" remains actively debated between nationalist and revisionist historians.
