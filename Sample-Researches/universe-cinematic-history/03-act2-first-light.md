# Act II — First Light: Nucleosynthesis & the Cosmic Microwave Background

**Scene: The universe's first three minutes. Chemistry begins.**

As the soup cooled, the first real particles formed. About three minutes after the Big Bang, in an era called **nucleosynthesis**, protons and neutrons fused into the first atomic nuclei — mostly **hydrogen** and **helium**, with traces of lithium and beryllium [8]. Within five minutes, most of today's helium (roughly a quarter of the universe's mass) had formed [8][10]. The universe was set: **~75% hydrogen, ~25% helium** [10].

But these were only *nuclei*. It was still far too hot for electrons to attach and form complete atoms. The universe was an **opaque fog** — light couldn't travel far because free electrons scattered it constantly [8].

## Recombination — the Climax (380,000 years after the Big Bang)

When the universe cooled to roughly 3,000°C, atomic nuclei finally captured electrons and became neutral atoms [8][17]. Two things happened at once:

1. The fog **cleared** — the universe became transparent for the first time.
2. The newly formed atoms released a glow of light.

That glow — stretched by 13.8 billion years of expansion to just **2.7 K above absolute zero** (−270°C) — still fills the sky today. It's called the **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)**: the **oldest light in the universe**, a baby picture taken 380,000 years after birth [8][10][14].

Satellites like COBE, WMAP, and Planck have mapped this ancient glow. Its tiny temperature ripples are the same seeds of structure that became today's galaxies [8][14].
