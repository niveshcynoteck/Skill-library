# Act III — The Cosmic Dark Ages & the First Stars

**Scene: Complete darkness. No stars, no galaxies. Only cold gas — and silence — for about 100 to 200 million years.**

After the CMB was released, the universe entered its **Dark Ages**. It was a sea of neutral hydrogen and helium, still cooling, with no source of light [8][11]. This is the only major era of cosmic history we have never directly observed [14].

## Cosmic Dawn — the first stars

Gravity did the work. Tiny overdensities of dark matter attracted hydrogen gas; the gas clumped, collapsed, and compressed until hydrogen began fusing. **The first stars ignited** somewhere around **100 to 200 million years after the Big Bang** [8][11].

These first-generation stars — called **Population III stars** — were unlike anything alive today:

- Made of **pure hydrogen and helium** — no "metals" (astronomers call every element heavier than helium a "metal") [12]
- **Enormous**: roughly **10 to 300 times the mass of the Sun** [12]
- **Blazing**: up to 100,000 K, hundreds of millions of times brighter than the Sun, shining mostly in ultraviolet [12][14]
- **Short-lived**: burning through their fuel in only a few million years, then dying in colossal supernovae [11][12]

## Reionization

The fierce UV light of the first stars split the neutral hydrogen back into protons and electrons, clearing the cosmic fog once more. This era, **reionization**, ran until the universe was roughly a billion years old [11].

Those first supernovae also did something vital: they **forged the first heavy elements** — carbon, oxygen, iron — and scattered them across space. Before then, these elements did not exist anywhere [12]. *Every heavy element in your body was made inside a star.* [10][19]

## The Hunt Today

No Population III star has been confirmed yet, but NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is now seeing deeper into Cosmic Dawn than any telescope before — discovering surprisingly early and bright galaxies and rewriting the textbooks [11][13].
